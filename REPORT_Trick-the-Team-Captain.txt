TITLE: Trick the Team Captain
OVERALL_COMMENT: You have created an interesting story with a clear problem and a satisfying ending, and Summer’s conflict with Tommy keeps the reader curious. Your strongest feature is the use of dialogue and small actions such as “bit her lip” and “smirked” to show the characters’ feelings. To raise your mark fastest, work on making your sentences clearer and checking punctuation, spelling and grammar carefully, especially when several ideas are joined together.

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: It was at Wednesday morning
CORRECT: It was on Wednesday morning
DESCRIPTION: We use “on” with days of the week. “At” is not correct here.
[/MISTAKE]

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: stern looking
CORRECT: stern-looking
DESCRIPTION: When two words work together as an adjective before a noun, a hyphen is often needed: “a stern-looking teacher.”
[/MISTAKE]

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: Tommy Miltons Bob
CORRECT: Tommy Milton’s Bob
DESCRIPTION: A possessive name needs an apostrophe. However, this sentence is also unclear because “Bob” seems to be an extra or incorrect name.
[/MISTAKE]

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: a rude boy and was in her team
CORRECT: a rude boy who was in her team
DESCRIPTION: “A rude boy and was” does not fit grammatically into the sentence. Use “who was” to connect the description correctly.
[/MISTAKE]

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: who was going to try and get the place to win
CORRECT: who was going to try to win the place
DESCRIPTION: “Try to win the place” is the clearer and more natural expression. “Get the place to win” changes the meaning.
[/MISTAKE]

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: Since no one else was in
CORRECT: Since no one else was in the running
DESCRIPTION: “Was in” is unclear here. “Was in the running” clearly means that nobody else was competing for the position.
[/MISTAKE]

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: As Summer’s special BFF, who was called Alice, helped her practice
CORRECT: As Summer’s special BFF, Alice, helped her practise
DESCRIPTION: The original sentence is awkward because “who was called Alice” is unnecessary. “Alice” can simply be placed between commas. In British English, “practise” is the verb used for practising a skill.
[/MISTAKE]

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: Alice cried “Where is Summer?”
CORRECT: Alice cried, “Where is Summer?”
DESCRIPTION: A comma should usually be placed before direct speech after a reporting verb such as “cried”.
[/MISTAKE]

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: Tommy made a sly smile
CORRECT: Tommy gave a sly smile
DESCRIPTION: We normally say someone “gave” or “smiled slyly”; we do not usually say someone “made a sly smile.”
[/MISTAKE]

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: Alice shouted “She’s really sick.”
CORRECT: Alice shouted, “She’s really sick.”
DESCRIPTION: A comma is needed before the direct speech.
[/MISTAKE]

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: annouced
CORRECT: announced
DESCRIPTION: “Announced” has two “n”s after the “a”. This is a spelling mistake.
[/MISTAKE]

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: labled
CORRECT: labelled
DESCRIPTION: The correct British English spelling is “labelled”, with two “l”s and two “l”s in the middle/end pattern.
[/MISTAKE]

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: Amile
CORRECT: Amile
DESCRIPTION: This name may be intended to be “Amile”, so it is not necessarily a spelling mistake. However, make sure character names remain consistent and are clearly introduced.
[/MISTAKE]

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: bloned
CORRECT: blonde
DESCRIPTION: The correct spelling is “blonde” when describing a girl or woman with fair/yellow hair.
[/MISTAKE]

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: shouted “Tommy made her sick.
CORRECT: shouted, “Tommy made her sick.”
DESCRIPTION: Add a comma before the direct speech.

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: People were talking. A few days later, Summer won.
CORRECT: People were talking. A few days later, Summer won the election.
DESCRIPTION: “Summer won” is grammatically possible, but the ending is too vague. Naming what she won makes the conclusion clearer.

[MISTAKE]
WRONG: Why she won was because Tommy got consenquenses
CORRECT: She won because Tommy faced consequences
DESCRIPTION: “Why she won was because” is an awkward sentence structure. Also, “consenquenses” is misspelled; the correct spelling is “consequences.”

CORRECT: Tommy stopped being mean after that.
DESCRIPTION: This sentence is correct. It works well as a simple ending, although you could make it more interesting by showing how Tommy changed.

[REFERENCE_WRITING]
Trick the Team Captain

It was Wednesday morning when the stern-looking class teacher asked who wanted to be the team captain. Summer, a girl with bright pink hair, nervously bit her lip. She hated speaking in front of thousands of people, yet she desperately didn’t want Tommy Milton’s Bob, the rude boy on her team, to become captain and try to lead them to victory. Since nobody else volunteered, Summer knew she would have to try.

As Summer’s best friend, Alice, helped her practise, Tommy called out confidently, “I’ll let you win.” Alice nodded, but Summer narrowed her eyes. “If this is one of his tricks, he won’t fool me,” she thought. The weekend passed in a blur, and before she knew it, the day of the competition had arrived.

A large group of children lined up outside the hall. Strangely, the only person inside was Tommy. Alice glanced around anxiously. “Where is Summer?” she cried. Tommy’s lips curled into a sly smile.

“Tommy, I should have known it was you!” Alice shouted.

“She’s really sick,” Tommy replied, trying to hide his smirk.

Just then, the hall door swung open and Mrs Cherry, a kind-hearted teacher, came rushing towards them. She announced, “Summer is sick.”

Suddenly, a small bottle labelled “Sick” slipped from Tommy’s pocket and clattered onto the floor. Amile, a girl with blonde hair, pointed at it in shock. “Tommy made her sick!”

Immediately, everyone began whispering and talking. Tommy’s cruel trick had been uncovered.

A few days later, Summer was announced as the winner. Tommy had faced the consequences of what he had done, while Summer had received more votes. From that day onwards, Tommy stopped being mean, and Summer had learnt that sometimes standing up to someone is the bravest thing of all.
[/REFERENCE_WRITING]

[LEARNING_POINT]
DESCRIPTION: Use a stronger, more precise adjective instead of a simple word such as “pink”. This gives the reader a clearer picture without adding lots of extra words.
ORIGINAL: Summer, a girl with pink hair, bit her lip.
BETTER_VERSION: Summer, a girl with bright pink hair, nervously bit her lip.
[/LEARNING_POINT]

[LEARNING_POINT]
DESCRIPTION: Vary your sentence structure by combining ideas with words such as “yet”, “although” or “while”. This makes your writing flow more naturally and avoids a series of simple sentences.
ORIGINAL: She hated speaking in front of thousands of people, but at the same time, she desperately didn’t want Tommy Miltons Bob, a rude boy and was in her team who was going to try and get the place to win.
BETTER_VERSION: She hated speaking in front of thousands of people, yet she desperately didn’t want Tommy Milton’s Bob, the rude boy on her team, to become captain and try to lead them to victory.
[/LEARNING_POINT]

[LEARNING_POINT]
DESCRIPTION: Show a character’s thoughts through a small action as well as dialogue. This helps the reader understand the character’s feelings instead of simply being told them.
ORIGINAL: Alice nodded, but Summer thought, “If this is his trick, he won’t fool me.”
BETTER_VERSION: Alice nodded, but Summer narrowed her eyes. “If this is one of his tricks, he won’t fool me,” she thought.
[/LEARNING_POINT]

[LEARNING_POINT]
DESCRIPTION: Use varied sentence openers to control the pace of your story. Openers such as “Strangely”, “Just then” and “Suddenly” can make important moments stand out.
ORIGINAL: The only person in there was Tommy.
BETTER_VERSION: Strangely, the only person inside was Tommy.
[/LEARNING_POINT]

[LEARNING_POINT]
DESCRIPTION: Add a small sensory detail at an important moment to make the action feel real. One carefully chosen sound or movement can make a discovery much more dramatic.
ORIGINAL: Just then, a bottle labled “sick” fell out of Tommy’s pocket.
BETTER_VERSION: Suddenly, a small bottle labelled “Sick” slipped from Tommy’s pocket and clattered onto the floor.

BETTER_VERSION: Suddenly, a small bottle labelled “Sick” slipped from Tommy’s pocket and clattered onto the floor. Amile, a girl with blonde hair, pointed at it in shock. “Tommy made her sick!”
